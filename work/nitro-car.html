Nitro 4WD car

A glow-engine, four-wheel-drive radio-controlled car built from its parts, with the engine, clutch, fuel system and radio all set up by hand.

  • 2015built, tuned and run over about a year
  • 4WDshaft drive through a front and a rear differential
  • 51distinct parts in the reconstruction, 81 pieces placed
  • 2radio channels: steering, and throttle with brake

Over about a year, in 2015, I built a nitro radio-controlled car: a four-wheel-drive chassis driven by a two-stroke glow engine through a centrifugal clutch, two differentials and a shaft. Nothing on it is plug-and-play. The gear mesh, the brake, the carburettor needles and both linkages are all set by hand, and the engine tells you at once when one of them is wrong. This page takes the car apart again, in drawings.

01Why a nitro car

An electric model car is a battery, a controller and a motor. A nitro car is a small machine with all the problems of a large one: an engine that needs the right mixture and temperature, a clutch that has to bite at the right speed, a fuel system with no pump, gears that strip if the mesh is loose, and a radio that has to survive fuel, oil and vibration.

That is why I built one. Every subsystem can be seen, taken apart and adjusted, and each adjustment changes how the car runs.

03Every part, numbered

The exploded view separates the car into its parts and numbers each one against the list underneath. The list has two halves: what is drawn, and what a complete build also needs but is too small or too loose to draw, from E-clips to the fuel bottle.

  • Chassis and suspension. A moulded tub, an upper deck, two shock towers, four lower arms with camber links, and four oil-filled shocks.
  • Drivetrain. A spur gear and brake on a layshaft, a centre shaft, a differential at each end and four driveshafts.
  • Engine. Crankcase, cooling head, carburettor and air filter, with the flywheel, clutch and clutch bell on the crankshaft.
  • Fuel and exhaust. Tank, two silicone lines, manifold and tuned pipe.
  • Radio. Receiver and battery in a sealed box, two servos and their linkages.
  • Wheels. Four tyres glued to six-spoke wheels, each held by one lock nut.

04How it runs

The glow plug is heated by a battery only to start. After that the heat of each combustion keeps its coil glowing, and the engine runs with no ignition system at all. Fuel reaches the carburettor because the exhaust pressurises the tank, so the mixture depends on throttle, on the needles and on every joint in the fuel lines being airtight.

Below a few thousand revolutions a minute the clutch shoes stay in and the car sits still with the engine idling. As the engine speeds up they fly outward, grip the clutch bell and the car pulls away. One servo handles both ends of this: forward opens the carburettor, back closes it and then squeezes the brake.

05Build order

The order matters because parts trap other parts. The differentials go into the gearboxes before the gearboxes go onto the tub; the clutch goes onto the engine before the engine goes in; the radio goes in last because its linkages can only be set once the carburettor and the brake are in place. The kit did not include a radio. I bought the transmitter, receiver and two servos separately, which meant choosing them, fitting them and setting them up myself; the next section covers that.

The last step is not assembly. A new engine is run through several tanks of fuel with a rich mixture and little throttle, so the piston and sleeve wear in against each other before they are asked for full power.

06The radio, bought separately

The kit came without a radio, so I bought one on its own: a two-channel pistol-grip transmitter, its receiver and two standard servos. That put the last part of the build in my hands. Nothing about a separate radio is plug-and-play: it has to be fitted, paired with its receiver and then taught the limits of this particular car.

  • Fit. The steering servo mounts at the front, the throttle and brake servo beside the engine. The receiver and its battery pack go in the sealed box, wrapped in foam against vibration, with the antenna led up the tube.
  • Bind. Receiver and transmitter are paired once, so the car answers this transmitter and no other on the same band.
  • Centre. With both trims at zero, the servo horns go on square. The steering link is then adjusted until the wheels point straight, and the throttle link until the carburettor sits at idle.
  • Direction. If turning the wheel right steers left, or pulling the trigger closes the throttle, the channel is reversed with a switch.
  • Travel. End points are set so that full lock does not strain the servo against the stops, full trigger opens the carburettor completely, and pushing forward closes it before the brake bites.
  • Fail-safe. If the signal is lost, the throttle servo returns to closed with the brake on, backed by the return spring on the carburettor.

The first drive is slow, with dual rate turned down so the steering is gentle while the trims are finished by eye.

07Time on the bench

I did not keep a log, so these are estimates from memory and from what is usual for the class. Assembly is the smaller half. The engine took longer than any mechanical stage, because break-in cannot be hurried and tuning is done one small needle turn at a time, with a run between each.
