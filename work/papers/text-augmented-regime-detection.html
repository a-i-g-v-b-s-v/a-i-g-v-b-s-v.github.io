Text-Augmented Regime Detection for Fixed Income Macro

Alexander Villax
University of Chicago Booth School of Business

Abstract

This note describes a three-layer system for detecting macroeconomic regimes and trading government bonds on them. The first layer labels the regime from public data using rules a person can read. The second uses a language model to turn central bank communication, news, interviews, earnings calls and policymakers' posts into dated, numerical scores. The third combines the two into positions in Treasury futures and curve trades. The contribution is in the design: regimes that are observable by construction, text that is timestamped to first publication, and macro data used only in the vintage that existed on the day. The note sets out the method, the tests it must pass, and a first test of layer one alone on public data from 1960 to 2025. The text layers are not yet tested.

1. The problem

Bond returns depend on the regime. A duration position that pays in a disinflationary slowdown loses in an inflation scare, and a model that averages over both learns little about either. Regime-switching models address this, beginning with Hamilton (1989) and extended to interest rates by Ang and Bekaert (2002). In those models the regime is a hidden state, inferred from the data and interpreted afterwards. That creates a practical difficulty. A portfolio manager asked to act on "regime two" will want to know what regime two is, and the answer is only a set of fitted means and variances.

Bie, Diebold, He and Li (2025) offer a way out. They define regimes with a decision tree on observable macro variables and let the tree choose the splits that best explain the dynamics of the yield curve, represented by the level, slope and curvature factors of Diebold and Li (2006). Every regime is then a rule. Their central finding is that macro variables add predictive content for yields in the high-rate regime and little in the others. This note takes two things from that paper: regimes should be defined on observables, and the value of a macro signal is itself regime-dependent.

2. Layer one: regimes as rules

The system uses five regimes: expansion with anchored inflation; late cycle with inflation drifting up; recession or risk-off; recovery with disinflation; and stagflation. Each is defined by thresholds on public series. A recession call, for example, needs the Sahm (2019) indicator at or above 0.5, or a spike in jobless claims, or high-yield spreads above the 95th percentile of the past year. No official recession dates enter.

Three design decisions matter. First, thresholds are taken from the literature and from policy targets, not estimated. With perhaps five true regime breaks since 1990, an estimated threshold is a fitted one. Each is then varied by 20% either way to check that the labels are stable. Second, the primary regime has few states. Stacking growth, inflation, credit and liquidity states multiplies into dozens of regimes that no sample could identify, so credit and liquidity enter as features and not as further states. Third, the word regime covers three different things: the economic regime, the market regime and the policy regime. They are kept as three separate variables because they are measured from different data and matter for different layers.

A supervised tree classifier is trained on the rule-based labels and is the model used downstream. A Markov-switching model in the spirit of Hamilton is fitted alongside it as a check: where the latent states and the labelled regimes disagree, that is something to investigate, not something to average away.

3. Data that does not know the future

Unemployment, output and inflation are revised, sometimes substantially, and a regime label built on today's revised figures knows things the market did not (Croushore, 2011). Every macro observation is therefore stored with two dates, the period it describes and the day it was published, and a backtest may only see the vintage available on the day it is trading. This was built before any label, because adding it afterwards means recomputing all of them.

4. Layer two: text as data

Policy is made in words before it appears in numbers. A large literature measures this: Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016) count newspaper language to build an index of policy uncertainty; Husted, Rogers and Sun (2020) do the same for monetary policy; Hansen and McMahon (2016) extract topics and tone from Federal Reserve statements; Cieslak and Vissing-Jorgensen (2021) read Fed minutes and transcripts to show how policymakers respond to the stock market. These methods rely on dictionaries and topic models. A language model can read for meaning: who is speaking, how certain they are, and whether what they say departs from what they said last time.

The system reads four tiers of source. The first is central banks and policymakers: statements, minutes, speeches, testimony and interviews. The second is the financial press and wire services, with bank research and newsletters. The third is earnings calls from companies that see the economy early, such as banks, freight, industrials and retailers. The fourth is the public posts of officials and economists. For each document the model returns the same seven fields: a hawkish-dovish score, growth sentiment, an inflation signal, credit stress, a geopolitical flag, the certainty of forward guidance, and who is speaking.

Three rules protect the result. Each document carries the time it first became public, not the time it was retrieved. The model version is pinned and every extraction is cached, so that a rerun gives the same series and a change of model cannot silently change history. And a fixed benchmark set of documents is rescored periodically to detect drift.

5. Layer three: from regime to position

The trading layer holds Treasury futures along the curve, curve steepeners and flatteners, inflation breakevens and sovereign spreads. Text scores are aggregated into daily series and enter a random forest together with the macro features (Breiman, 2001). Forests are suited to this because there are many candidate inputs and weak priors on most of them; where theory is clear, the relationship is constrained to be monotonic (Gholkar and Sueppel, 2024). Positions are sized to a volatility target, and the size of any macro or text signal depends on the regime, following the finding that such signals are informative in some regimes and not in others.

6. How it is tested

The sample runs from 1960 to 2025 for the regime labels. The trading backtest must include, out of sample, three breaks of different kinds: 2007 to 2009, 2020 and 2022. Validation follows the standard I use elsewhere: walk-forward folds with no leakage, a block bootstrap for confidence intervals, and a permutation test against a no-skill null. A result that does not survive all three is not reported as a result.

Expectations are set in advance. Diversified monthly macro signals on liquid markets have historically delivered Sharpe ratios of roughly 1.0 to 1.5 as a portfolio, and 0.3 to 0.7 on a single market. A figure far above that range is more likely a leak than a discovery.

7. A first test of layer one

The rule-based classifier was run unchanged on public data: labour data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, core PCE from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, industrial production and constant-maturity Treasury yields from the Federal Reserve. Labels run monthly from June 1960 to December 2025. At each month-end the classifier sees labour and production data for the previous month and core inflation for the month before that. Two inputs are proxies: the Baa spread over the ten-year yield stands in for high-yield spreads, and before 2003, when no breakeven exists, long-run inflation expectations are proxied by five-year trailing core inflation. The series are current vintages with publication lags, not archived first releases.

The positions were fixed in advance from economic reasoning: long the ten-year bond in recession, half long in expansion and recovery, half short late in the cycle, short in stagflation, scaled to a 10% volatility target, with 2 basis points of cost per unit of turnover. Over 731 months from 1965 to 2025 this earned a Sharpe ratio of 0.35 (block-bootstrap 95% interval 0.11 to 0.59), against 0.17 for a constant long position at the same scaling. Adding a duration-neutral 2s10s curve trade gives 0.47. Shifting the regime path in time 5,000 ways gives a p-value of 0.01. Moving every threshold by up to 20% leaves the Sharpe ratio between 0.26 and 0.35. In 2022 the strategy returned 8% while the constant long position lost 13%.

The test also exposed faults. The recession regime covers 33% of months, far more than official recessions, because the monthly credit proxy fires on any new twelve-month high. High but falling inflation is labelled as anchored expansion. The average spell lasts 4.3 months, which is too short. And positions estimated walk-forward from each regime's past returns do worse (0.18) than positions stated from priors. These results are a baseline for the text layers to beat, not a claim about the full system.

8. Limits

The number of regime breaks is small, so statistical power is low and economic priors carry weight. Text history is uneven: transcripts thin out before 2010 and social-media archives are costly, so central bank speeches serve as the long historical record. Language models change, which is why extractions are cached and versions pinned. And the regime rules, however carefully chosen, are choices; the sensitivity analysis shows how much depends on them, but does not remove the dependence.
