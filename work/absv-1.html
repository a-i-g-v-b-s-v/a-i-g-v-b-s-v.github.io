ABSV-1

A 1:20 scale PBY Catalina flying boat with two motors, built up from its individual parts, wired, and set up to fly from water.

ABSV-1 is a radio-controlled model of the Consolidated PBY Catalina, the twin-engined flying boat of the 1930s and 40s. It spans 1,470 mm, carries two brushless motors turning in opposite directions, and takes off from and lands on water. I put it together from its separate parts in 2017: the foam airframe, the motors and speed controllers, four servos, a receiver with a built-in gyro, and the wiring between them. This page covers the airframe, the power system, the controls, and the twelve-step radio setup that has to be right before the first flight.

01The aircraft

The Catalina is an unusual shape for good reasons, and that is why I chose it. The hull is a boat. The wing sits high above it on a pylon so that the propellers and the wing itself stay clear of spray, and two struts each side carry the load back down to the hull. Floats under the wingtips stop a wing from digging into the water.

All of that carries over to the model, and it makes the build more demanding than a land aircraft of the same size: two motors to wire and match, a wing in three pieces on spar tubes, and electronics that have to live a few centimetres above water.

02The airframe

The airframe is moulded EPO foam, which is light, takes a knock without snapping, and floats. The hull has a step in its planing bottom under the wing, as the real one does: at speed the water breaks away at the step, the drag falls, and the aircraft can lift off.

The wing comes as a middle section with both motor nacelles and two outer panels. Fibreglass tubes run through all three and take the bending load, so the foam only has to hold its shape. The tail sits high on the fin, out of the spray, on a tube of its own.

03Power: two motors, turning opposite ways

Each motor has its own speed controller, and both controllers draw from one battery through a Y-harness I soldered. The throttle signal reaches both through a second Y-lead, so one stick moves them together. One of the controllers also supplies 5 V to the receiver and the servos.

The two motors turn in opposite directions, with a mirrored pair of propellers. A single propeller twists the aircraft the other way and pulls it off line as power comes on, which matters most on water, where there are no wheels to hold a heading. Two propellers turning against each other cancel that out.

The order of the three wires between a controller and its motor sets the direction. Getting one motor wrong gives a propeller that blows forwards, so both are checked at low throttle before a propeller is ever fitted tight.

04Controls and the receiver

Four small servos move the control surfaces: one in each wing for the ailerons, and two in the hull for the elevator and rudder, working through steel pushrods. The two aileron servos share one channel on a Y-lead.

The receiver contains a six-axis gyro. It senses the aircraft rolling, pitching or yawing when no command was given, and moves the surfaces to oppose it. That only works if it is mounted flat and square, and if every channel is set the right way round: a gyro correcting in the wrong direction makes a gust worse.

05Setting up the radio

A new transmitter and receiver know nothing about the aircraft. Everything is set once and stored in a model memory: which way each surface moves, how far, how sharply it responds, what the switches do, and what happens if the signal is lost.

The order matters. Directions come before the failsafe, because reversing a channel afterwards also reverses the failsafe position. Throws come before expo and rates, which are percentages of the throw.

06A flight, from water to water

A flying boat has no brakes and no wheels, so every flight starts by pointing the nose into the wind and opening the throttle smoothly. At first the hull ploughs through the water. As speed builds it rises onto its planing bottom, the step breaks the water's grip, and the aircraft accelerates quickly to flying speed.

In the air it flies a circuit that stays close enough to see which way it is pointing. Landing is the take-off in reverse: power back, a steady descent, and a gentle flare so the step touches first. The timer on the transmitter is what ends the flight, with charge to spare for a second approach.

07Failsafe, and the routine

The setting I consider most important is the failsafe. Out of the box, a lost signal makes the receiver hold its last command. If that command was half throttle, the aircraft flies on at half throttle with nobody in control. I changed the throttle failsafe to closed, so that losing the link stops both motors.

The other protection is habit. Throttle hold goes on before the battery is plugged in and stays on until the aircraft is pointed where it should go. After the battery goes in, the aircraft is left still while the gyro finds level.
