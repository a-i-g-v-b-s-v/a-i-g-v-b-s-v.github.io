A.R.I.S

A systematic macro overlay that trades commodity futures from a four-factor signal conditioned on the growth and inflation regime.

  • +2.3%return of the rules over the live period, 6 April to 23 October 2026, replayed from market prices
  • 22commodity futures across five sectors, re-ranked every day
  • 4risk checks that must all pass before any order reaches the broker
  • 3.7%a year in the engine's own fifteen-year backtest, with a 42% drawdown

A.R.I.S reads the macro regime from growth and inflation data, ranks 22 commodity futures on momentum, carry, value and reversal, sizes each position to a volatility target and sends the orders to Interactive Brokers behind four hard-coded risk checks. I ran it from April to October 2026 and then closed it. This page shows what it does, what its rules returned over those months, and where its results fall short of the coursework that inspired it.

01The idea

Commodity returns depend on where the economy is in the cycle. Energy and industrial metals do well when growth and inflation rise together; precious metals hold up when growth falls and inflation does not. A momentum model that ignores this buys the right contracts in the wrong regime.

A.R.I.S puts the two together. A multi-factor signal says which contracts are strong; a regime classifier says which sectors deserve more or less of that signal; a sizing layer and four risk checks decide how much, and whether, to trade.

02Four factors, ranked across the universe

Each contract gets four scores, and each score is a cross-sectional rank mapped to the range −1 to +1, so no single contract's scale can dominate.

  • Momentum, 40%. The twelve-month price return.
  • Carry, 25%. The roll yield, from the futures curve where it is available.
  • Value, 20%. Distance below the long-run mean price.
  • Reversal, 15%. The negative of the last month's return.

The final score averages that composite with a time-series trend score, and is then multiplied by the sector scalar for the regime in force.

03Regime: growth against inflation

The classifier takes two series from FRED, a growth indicator and consumer price inflation, and asks one question of each: is the six-month average rising or falling? Two answers give four quadrants.

Each quadrant carries a row of sector multipliers. They are scalars on the signal, not allocations: in Reflation a copper signal counts for 1.4 times its raw score, in Deflation for 0.7 times.

04Sizing and risk

Position size is the tilted score scaled by the ratio of a 10% volatility target to the contract's own 60-day volatility, then capped. Quiet contracts get more capital, violent ones get less.

Four checks run before any order, and all four must pass:

  • VIX below 35. Above it, no new entries.
  • Position cap. No order above the per-position limit.
  • Daily loss stop. Trading stops for the day at −3% of NAV.
  • Time filter. Nothing is sent in the last 30 minutes of the session.

The target is set per contract, so the portfolio as a whole is not held at 10%. In the backtest its volatility averaged about 15%.

05Connected to Interactive Brokers

A.R.I.S talks to Interactive Brokers through IB Gateway. It converts each target weight into a number of contracts, using micro contracts where the exchange lists them, compares that with what the account already holds and sends only the difference. The trade log records every line the pipeline considered and what became of it: first as dry runs, then as submitted and filled orders.

I ran it from April to October 2026 and have since closed it. I did not keep the account statements, so the figures here are not a broker record. They are a replay: the same rules, run by the project's own backtest engine over the same dates on public futures prices, with 2 basis points of cost on every trade.

Over those 141 trading days the rules return +2.3%, against +15.7% for a broad commodity index fund. The path was rough: after a good April the rules lost money through May and June, to a drawdown of 20.6%, and made it back from July onward, at an annualised volatility of 18%.

06From rehearsal to real orders

Before any order went out, the pipeline ran for days in dry-run mode: computing targets, converting them to contracts and writing every line it would have sent. The April trade log has 88 such lines. Seventy-eight are dry runs; the last ten are real, and three of those filled.

Each line records the contract month, the target as a share of net asset value, the multiplier and the price, so a position can be traced back to the score that produced it. Limit orders are cancelled if they have not filled within fifteen minutes.

07Failure handling

A system that runs unattended every morning will meet a missing data feed, a broker that will not connect and a git push that fails. Each case has a written response, and all of them lean the same way: when in doubt, do nothing and log it loudly.

After each run J.E.F.F.R.E.Y writes a one-page note from that day's data.

08Two backtests, two answers

The four-factor idea began as coursework: a monthly strategy on 24 commodities with licensed futures data, which reported a Sharpe ratio of 1.83 over 2002 to 2025 and 1.31 out of sample. That study has its own page, caveats included.

A.R.I.S is a different implementation: daily, on free continuous-contract prices, with costs. Its own engine, run over January 2011 to April 2026, returns 3.7% a year at 14.9% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.05, with a drawdown of 42% that lasted from 2022 into 2024. It made money in Reflation and lost it in Stagflation.

The gap between the two is the main finding of the project, and the charts here are the engine's, not the coursework's.

09An audit trail

A backtest is a claim. A live record is evidence, but only if nobody can go back and tidy it. So each run writes that day's signal file, risk file and trade log, and commits them to git with the reasoning recorded before the trade.

The public repository holds the runs from April 2026. The factor table above is one of those files, unchanged.

10What works today, and what does not

  • Works: the signal pipeline from FRED and price data to a target position file; the four risk checks; orders to Interactive Brokers; daily signal, risk and trade files; fund notes written from the day's data by J.E.F.F.R.E.Y, the assistant described elsewhere on this site.
  • The result is weak: a Sharpe ratio near zero over fifteen years and a 42% drawdown. Over the live period the rules made 2.3% while a plain commodity fund made far more.
  • Why, as far as I can tell: without real futures curves for the whole history, carry is partly a proxy; the growth series is a manufacturing proxy, not the ISM index; prices are continuous contracts that are not roll-adjusted; and the portfolio's total volatility is not controlled.
  • Not yet built: a portfolio-level volatility target, per-position stops, and flattening on a VIX halt.

11What comes next

Reconcile the two backtests properly: run the coursework signal and the A.R.I.S engine on the same data, with the same lags and costs, and find out how much of the difference is data and how much was never there.
