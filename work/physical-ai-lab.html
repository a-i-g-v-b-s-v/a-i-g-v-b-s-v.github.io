Physical AI Lab

A desk-mounted, two-armed robot with dexterous hands and 360° vision, built to learn what robot data, training and each success really take.

  • 2 × 7arm joints: two arms with seven degrees of freedom each
  • 3360° cameras: on the hub and on each shoulder, each seeing all the way round
  • 12finger joints per hand, six of them actuated
  • 8phases, November 2026 to May 2027

Robot-learning companies sell data, training and models. This project buys the experience from the other side of the table. The robot sits on a column clamped to my desk and turns 360° on its base; two arms carry Inspire RH56 dexterous hands, and three 360° cameras give it a view all the way round. A planner decides what to do and a learned policy decides how to move. Eight phases take it from a digital twin in November 2026 to measured results and a cost-per-task calculator.

01The end product

A robot that lives on my work desk and can help from any angle. A vertical column is clamped to the desk; at its foot, a slewing base lets the whole robot turn 360° on the spot. On top, a shoulder hub carries two arms, the controller and the emergency stop.

Each arm has seven joints, so a hand can reach around an object rather than only over it. The hands are Inspire Robots RH56-series dexterous hands: five fingers, six actuated joints driving twelve finger joints, with fingertip force sensing on the force-sensing variant.

02How it sees

The robot's eye is three 360° cameras: one on top of the hub and one on top of each shoulder. Each has two back-to-back fisheye lenses, so it sees all the way round, and global-shutter sensors, so the image stays sharp while the base turns. Because all three see everything, every point around the robot is seen from three places: that gives depth in every direction, and no blind spot if one camera fails.

For close work, a camera in each wrist watches the fingers, the view robot foundation models rely on most for grasping, and a stereo depth camera behind the hub's visor maps the desk to the millimetre. All cameras are calibrated to the robot with a ChArUco board and time-synchronised with the joint encoders, so an image and a joint angle recorded together really happened together.

Sensors give it touch and caution: fingertip force sensors in the hands, a six-axis force-torque sensor in each wrist to feel contact, current sensing in every motor to stop on a collision, a person-detection ring around the base that slows the arms when hands come near, a microphone array for voice commands, and an IMU in the hub.

03The brain

Two kinds of intelligence, kept separate. A planner, a language model, turns a request such as "clear the left side of the desk" into steps and calls skills like pick, place and hand over. The candidates are the Claude API, Nous Research's Hermes models, or an open model served on a rented NVIDIA GPU; the choice comes down to latency, reliability, privacy and cost per task.

Below it, a visuomotor policy turns the camera images, forces and joint states into motor commands 30 to 50 times a second. The first one is trained from scratch on my own demonstrations; the second is an open vision-language-action model fine-tuned on the same data on a rented NVIDIA GPU. Comparing the two on the same trials shows what a foundation model is worth in data and GPU time.

04From demonstration to result

The robot learns from demonstrations. I drive both arms and hands through VR hand tracking while the cameras, the force sensors and all 27 joints are recorded in the LeRobot format. Automatic checks reject bad episodes, and operator time is logged per usable episode, because that is the number a customer pays for.

05The plan, phase by phase

Eight phases, each ending in a test it must pass before the next begins. The first builds a digital twin, so reach, collisions and camera coverage are checked in simulation before anything is bought. The second is a small learning arm on Feetech STS3215 servos: the whole pipeline, cheaply, before the big robot exists. Those servos cannot hold a dexterous hand at desk reach, so the final arms use stronger actuators on a CAN bus.

06How success will be measured

Every policy and planner faces the same fixed protocol: 20 filmed trials per task, ten from starting positions seen in training and ten from new ones, judged by a written rule. The gap between seen and new positions shows whether the robot learned the task or memorised the demonstrations.

07The economics

The last deliverable turns the logs into a cost per task learned: operator time per usable demonstration, GPU-hours per fine-tune, planner calls per task and the hardware, amortised. It is the number a company deploying robots across many sites needs, built from one real robot.

08Safety and how the work is run

  • Safety first. A hardware emergency stop, current and torque limits in firmware, a soft workspace boundary, and slow speeds whenever a person's hands are in the workspace. No unattended runs until the safety checks pass.
  • Real or labelled. Success rates and timings come from real runs; drawings and plans are marked as such.
  • Check before commanding. Robot software changes fast, so every command is checked against the installed version and current documentation.
  • Gates. Each phase ends with a short demo and a decision.
  • Division of work. I design, build, wire and operate the robot; Claude Code writes the simulation, drivers, data pipeline, training and evaluation code, and these pages.
