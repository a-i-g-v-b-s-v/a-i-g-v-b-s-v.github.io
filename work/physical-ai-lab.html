Robot-learning companies sell data collection, training and models. This project buys the experience from the other side of the table: build an open-source SO-101 arm, teach it to pick up a cube and place it in a bin, and record what every step costs in operator time, GPU time and dollars. The parts list is done and the frame will be printed by hand; the rest follows in eleven milestones, each ending in one number, one video and a one-page write-up.

01What it measures

Companies building robot foundation models talk to customers about demonstrations, fine-tuning and success rates. To sell that well, I want to have done it: collected the data, trained the policy, judged the trials and paid the bills.

The task is deliberately small: pick up a cube and place it in a bin. Small enough to finish, real enough to show where the time and money go. Each milestone ends in one headline number, one video and a one-page write-up for a non-engineer.

02The hardware

The arm is the open-source SO-101 from TheRobotStudio, built in pairs: a leader that a person moves and a follower that copies it. I chose Seeed's servo kit, whose 12 V follower servos have almost twice the torque of the official parts list's, and I will print the frame myself.

The model below is drawn from the arm's published proportions: six servos per arm, with the follower carrying the gripper and a wrist camera and the leader a handle and trigger. It is a render of the design, not a photograph; photographs replace it once the arms are built.

04Printing it

The official files assume PLA+ on an FDM printer with a 220 mm bed. A fit gauge is printed first to test screw bite before committing a material to the arm links. The Formlabs Form 4 has a smaller build area, so it uses the repository's individual part files and is kept for precision parts: the custom gripper, camera adapters, and fingertips in flexible resin.

Assembly, motor setup and calibration follow the official LeRobot SO-101 guide step by step, and the cameras follow its camera guide. The project links to those guides instead of rewriting them.

05How the work is run

  • Results are real or labelled. Success rates, costs and timings come from real runs and logs; anything illustrative says so.
  • No spend without an estimate. Parts, GPU rental and paid APIs are approved one by one, with the expected cost first.
  • Everything that feeds the economics is logged: operator minutes per episode, reset time, rejected episodes, GPU type, GPU-hours and dollars.
  • The software moves fast, so every command is checked against the installed LeRobot version and the current documentation.
  • Milestones are gates. Each one ends with a summary and a decision before the next begins.
  • Division of work. I do the ordering, printing, assembly, CAD and operating of the arm; Claude Code writes the scripts, configs, data checks, training and evaluation code, cost log and web pages.

06Collecting the data

One task, defined tightly, with a written success rule that someone else could judge. Demonstrations start from a grid of cube positions, some of which are kept back for testing. Episodes have a fixed length, a reset procedure and lighting notes.

A quality check flags dropped frames, timeouts and failed demonstrations and prints the dataset's statistics. Operator time is logged per episode and per usable episode, because the second number is the one a customer pays for. The dataset goes to the Hugging Face Hub with a dataset card.

07How success will be measured

Every policy faces the same 20 filmed trials: ten from start positions seen in training and ten from positions held out. The gap between the two is the real test of whether it learned the task or memorised the demonstrations.

Results go into a table of success rates and failure modes, and the trials are cut into one clip with the success rate on screen.

08Three ways to teach the same task

The project compares three routes a customer could take: train a small policy (ACT) from scratch, call a hosted robot-model API, or fine-tune an open robot foundation model on a rented GPU. Each is judged on the same 20 trials, and on the data, GPU-hours and dollars it needed.

The hosted-API test is written up as customer feedback: onboarding friction, what worked, what broke, and what I would tell the product team. Before any code, I check that the service supports the SO-101 or a close equivalent.

09The economics

The last deliverables turn the logs into money. An interactive calculator gives the cost per usable demonstration hour and the cost per task learned, with charts showing which input matters most. A one-page write-up, What it cost to teach my robot one task, totals every GPU-hour and prices it against a public compute index, and explains how a company running thousands of fine-tunes would think about hedging that cost.

11Beyond the first task

After the first evaluation, the strongest additions are the ones that answer a customer's questions with little extra hardware time:

  • A data scaling curve: train on 10, 25, 50 and 100 episodes and plot success against data. How many demonstrations does one task cost?
  • Does data quality predict success? Score each episode for idle time, jerk and gripper chatter, and test whether dropping the worst fifth helps.
  • Cost per successful trial: the data side and the compute side joined in one number.
  • Further out: corrective data collection, automatic success judging by a vision-language model, the operator's learning curve, a reliability log turned into cost of ownership, and resin-printed sensorised fingers tested as a controlled variable.
