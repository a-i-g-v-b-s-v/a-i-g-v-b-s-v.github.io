Discretionary trading

Five years of trading my own account in shares, options and futures: themes chosen from the top down, entries set in advance, and positions sized to survive being wrong.

Alongside the systematic work I trade a personal account by judgment. It began with $10,000 in April 2021 and stood at about $78,000 in November 2026, across 282 trades that are all listed on this page. The method is simple to state: find a bottleneck the market is paying for, map the companies along it, write down the price at which each is worth owning, and wait. Options express the views with a clear edge and a date; futures carry the macro ones. This page gives the full record first, losses included, and then the method behind it.

01The record, 2021 to 2026

The ledger has 282 positions: 142 in shares, 93 in listed options, 26 in futures, and a few in invoice finance and private companies. The account started with $10,000 in April 2021 and stood at $78,012 on 14 November 2026, with no further deposits.

The path was not smooth. The first two years went nowhere: I bought call options into the 2022 bear market and the account fell 47% from its high. Most of the growth came from 2023 on. 140 of 270 closed trades made money, which is barely more than half; the result comes from the winners being larger than the losers, $105,080 against $45,506.

05How my trading has changed

I started in 2021 with one idea: buy call options on companies I believed in. Of the 11 trades I opened that year, 9 were calls. It was a concentrated, expensive way to be long the market, and 2022 showed me what that means. The calls lost together, the hedges came late, and the account fell 47% from its high.

What I changed first was simple. I bought shares where I wanted to own the company and kept options for views with a date. By 2024 three trades in four were shares, and options were under a quarter.

The second change came when I started studying quantitative finance properly at Chicago Booth, which means the mathematics and statistics underneath the markets I had been trading by instinct.

  • Probability and statistics. Regression, time series and resampling taught me to ask whether a result is a pattern or luck, and how many trades it takes to tell the difference.
  • Portfolio mathematics. Covariance matrices and mean-variance optimisation showed me that risk is a property of the whole book. Ten positions that move together are one position.
  • Option pricing. Deriving Black-Scholes from stochastic calculus made the Greeks concrete: what an option gains from movement, what it loses each day, and why a hedged option is a position in volatility and not in direction.
  • Fixed income. Duration, convexity and the yield curve gave me a way to measure exposure exactly and to hedge it with a second instrument.
  • Carry and curves. A futures price already contains storage, financing and dividends. The shape of the curve pays or costs something every day, whatever my view is.
  • Market microstructure. Spreads, market impact and execution models showed me that the cost of trading is part of the return and can be estimated in advance.

The effect was that I stopped thinking only about what to buy and started thinking about how a position is built: what it earns while I wait, what it is exposed to besides my view, and what hedges it. In 2026, 38 of the 74 trades I opened were futures, relative-value positions, hedges or invoice advances, kinds of trade I did not know how to make in 2021.

The core has not changed. I still pick themes from the top down, set a buying level for every name in advance, and keep each position small enough that being wrong is cheap. The chart of buy levels is that list on one day: ten names at or under their level, seventeen still waiting.

08Futures: going to the source

In 2026 I started trading futures, beginning with agriculture. When the United States and China announced a trade deal with farm purchases in it, I wanted to know whether prices on the ground had moved. So I called ranchers and farmers in Texas and asked what they were being offered for cattle and for crops.

The answers shaped the trades: long live cattle, where supply was already tight, and long wheat. Cattle worked. Wheat was early: the buyers the headline promised did not arrive in size.

Metals were the weak spot. Gold made money once, on the Monday after the Gulf closed, and lost on the other attempts; platinum and silver lost. Crude and heating oil came later, with the war.
