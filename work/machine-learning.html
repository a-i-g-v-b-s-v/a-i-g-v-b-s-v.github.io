Timing factors with machine learning

Can macro, market and news data say when value, momentum and other factors will pay? Six model types, one out-of-sample test.

  • 5factors predicted: value, momentum, quality, investment and size
  • 18inputs: interest rates, market conditions, factor returns and news sentiment
  • 93test months, after training on the first 216
  • 3 of 5factors where timing beat simply holding the factor, by Sharpe ratio

Equity factors such as value and momentum earn their premium unevenly: years of nothing, then a burst. This project asks whether a model can tell the two apart in advance. It predicts next month's return on five factors from 18 macro, market and news-sentiment inputs, with linear models and tree ensembles, and tests each on 93 months it never saw. The short answer is: barely.

01The question

Factor investing asks which stocks to hold. Factor timing asks when to hold the factor at all. If value does well when rates are rising and badly when markets are calm and narrow, a model that reads those conditions could step aside for the bad stretches.

The literature is sceptical that this works, which makes it a good test for machine learning: a problem with real economic content, little data and a strong chance of fooling yourself.

02Can the models forecast factor returns?

Not by the strictest measure. Out-of-sample R² is negative for every model on every factor: none does better than forecasting the average. The linear models are worst, because with 18 correlated inputs and 216 months they fit noise. The random forest comes closest to zero.

Rank correlation is kinder. For size and investment the forecasts line up with outcomes at 0.1 to 0.2, which is useful if it is real. For value and momentum it is close to nothing.

03Does timing make money?

Turning forecasts into positions gives a mixed result. Timing raised the Sharpe ratio for value and momentum, and reduced the losses on size. It made quality and investment worse. That is 3 of five, over 93 months, with hit rates between 41% and 56%.

The awkward part is that the two factors where timing helped most are the two where the forecasts had the least rank correlation. Value and momentum were simply weak over the test window, and a rule that was out of the market part of the time looked good. That is luck in the sample at least as much as skill in the model.

04What the models looked at

The tree models lean most on the state of the market: volatility over the past year, how dispersed stock returns are, and the market's own return. Interest rates come next. The four news-based inputs together carry about 17% of the importance, enough to matter and not enough to lead.

05What I take from it

  • The result is a negative one, and that is worth having. With a few hundred months of data, flexible models do not find a reliable timing signal in these inputs.
  • Method mattered more than model. Splitting in time order, validating on an expanding window and comparing with buy-and-hold are what stopped a weak result from looking like a strong one.
  • Where to look next: the two factors with real rank correlation, size and investment, and a longer sample that covers more rate cycles.
