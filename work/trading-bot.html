Trading Bot

An automated trader for a simulated exchange: two market makers, an options volatility trade across six strikes, and a delta hedge, written for the IMC Prosperity competition.

IMC Prosperity is a trading competition in which each team submits one program that trades against the exchange's own bots. I finished 104th of 18,803. This page is the third round, where the products were two spot instruments and ten call options on one of them. The bot does three things at once: it makes markets where the spread is wide, it trades the options against each other where the volatility smile is out of line, and it hedges what is left.

01Reading the market first

Before writing any strategy I measured the data: three days, ten thousand snapshots a day for each product. Two facts decided the design. Both spot products reverse from one tick to the next, which is what someone providing liquidity wants. And one of them has a spread of sixteen ticks, so the quotes in the book are a poor guide to fair value and there is room to quote inside them.

02The option that was too cheap

I solved for the implied volatility of every option at every tick. Across the six tradable strikes it sits close to 20% and barely moves. One strike is the exception: fitted against a smile drawn through the others, the 5400 call is cheap by about 1.4 volatility points, more than 99% of the time.

That became a relative-value trade: buy the strikes that are cheap to the smile, sell the ones that are rich, and hedge the net exposure in the underlying. It does not need a view on where the price goes.

03Eight submissions

The exchange scores each submission on a short window. The first lost 3,328 because of a position limit I had set wrong. Every later change was a response to what the logs showed, and two of them made things worse before I reverted them.

04What the backtest got wrong

My own backtester put the profit at about 380,000 over three days. The exchange's results were a small fraction of that. The difference is the fill model: I assumed my resting orders would be filled a quarter of the time the price touched them, and on the real exchange the other bots are far less generous.

I kept the number on this page because the lesson is the useful part. A market-making backtest is only as good as its assumption about who trades with you, and that is the one thing historical data does not contain.
