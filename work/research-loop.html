Research Loop

The research process of a systematic fund, built end to end and applied to the academic literature. 230 strategies tested; two survive.

Most backtests overstate results because the researcher keeps trying variations until one works. The Research Loop takes that freedom away. An idea goes in, becomes an exact rule, and has to pass every check at once, with every attempt counted, so each new try raises the bar. I ran 230 strategies through it: fourteen famous ones, all 212 stock anomalies published in the academic literature, combinations of them, and the ideas of an AI researcher. Two anomalies survive.

01Thesis

The problem. Every systematic investor faces the same trap: a backtest tells you as much about how many ideas you tried as about the idea itself. Try enough variations and something always looks good. AI tools that turn an idea into a backtested strategy in seconds make this worse, because they try thousands. The scarce thing is not ideas. It is a trustworthy way to reject them.

What I built. The research process of a systematic fund, end to end, as software. An idea, from a paper, a sentence or an AI model, becomes an exact rule. The rule is backtested with realistic trading costs and judged by a fixed referee: out-of-sample tests, a penalty that grows with every idea ever tried, placebo tests on scrambled data, factor attribution and capacity limits. Only what survives goes to paper trading, where the same rule runs and is reviewed every month.

What it found. 230 strategies went through it, including all 212 stock anomalies published in the academic literature. Two passed every check. One, cash-based operating profitability, was then tested in 16 countries it had never seen and held up in 15; it now runs in a simulated $1,000,000 book that rebalances itself monthly. The rest failed honestly, including momentum, value and size, and an AI researcher whose ideas looked strong in development and faded on sealed data. The system also caught four errors in my own work, each of which had produced a false winner.

What it can do.

  • Test any idea the same way: a paper, a factor, a portfolio or AI-generated code, with every attempt counted.
  • Tell real edge from luck: out-of-sample and walk-forward tests, deflated Sharpe ratios, placebo tests, false-discovery control.
  • Say whether it is tradable: per-stock spreads, market impact, borrowing costs, and how much capital it can take before the edge disappears.
  • Keep AI honest: sandboxed code, look-ahead detection, novelty checks against published research, sealed holdouts.
  • Run it: paper trading on the same rule object, automated monthly rebalancing, and a review that stops the strategy if it stops validating.
  • Be checked by someone else: automated tests on every change, a searchable record of every trial, and every input documented.

Why it matters. The value of a research platform is not the strategies it finds but the bad ones it stops. This project shows I can understand a complex financial product down to its data and statistics, build it end to end, and explain plainly what it does and does not do.

02How a strategy is judged

A strategy survives only if it passes every check at once. Each targets a different way a backtest can mislead: fitting the past, one lucky period, being one good draw among many, profiting from noise, or earning what known factors already pay. The last two rows are what an allocator asks first: is there alpha, and how much money can it take.

What a strategy must pass, all at once

04212 published anomalies

Then every stock-return predictor catalogued by Open Source Asset Pricing, run through the identical test on the 1,000 largest US stocks with trading costs. The typical anomaly earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.05 over its history and −0.05 after its paper was published. On the papers' own portfolios, returns after publication fall to 46% of their in-sample level, close to what McLean and Pontiff found in 2016. A stronger result in the original paper does not predict survival: the correlation is −0.12.

Two survive. Cash-based operating profitability is the convincing one: known factors explain only a quarter of it, and it keeps working after publication and at $100m with realistic costs. Earnings streaks pass on raw returns but are largely explained by known factors.

05The survivor, now paper trading

Cash-based operating profitability was rebuilt directly from Compustat, line by line from the paper's definition. It matches the published signal with a rank correlation of 0.985, then went through the full filter again with all 390 trials counted. It passes both tests: a test-half Sharpe of 0.78, walk-forward 0.66 with all five periods positive, alpha of 6.1% a year after the five factors and momentum (t = 4.4). With realistic costs it keeps 0.52 at $10m, 0.47 at $100m and 0.28 at $1bn.

So it trades. A $1,000,000 simulated book, 76 long and 75 short across the 1,000 largest US stocks, opened on 13 November 2026 and rebalances itself after every month-end, through the same gate: if the strategy ever stops passing validation, the paper step refuses to trade it. The record builds month by month from here.

06Out of sample abroad

Every strategy here was discovered on US data, so the cleanest remaining test is to look where nobody looked. Using Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen's global factor returns, I took the survivor and four classic factors to 19 markets outside the US, with trading costs estimated from the US tests, and counted all 100 tests as trials.

The survivor travels: cash-based operating profitability is positive after costs in 15 of 16 countries, with alpha above three standard errors in 9, and it passes every check for the world outside the US as a whole (net Sharpe 0.49, walk-forward 1.08). The classics do not: size is positive in 1 of 16 countries, momentum in 12, and short-term reversal in none after costs.

07Combining them

Funds blend many weak signals into one score. Done naively, a composite of all 212 signals earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.74 with strong alpha. But that uses signals before they were discovered: in 1980 only 4 were public. Using each signal only from the year after its paper appeared gives 0.31, and about 0.00 at $100m once spreads and market impact are charged. Hindsight is worth more than any signal.

08Momentum, decade by decade

Momentum is the most famous anomaly. It reproduces its 1993 paper on the paper's own sample, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.78 in 1965–1989, then fades: 0.26 over 1990–2024, with a 72% drawdown in the 2009 crash. Volatility scaling halves the drawdown but adds no return out of sample. With realistic costs it falls to 0.19 at $10m and about zero at $1bn.

09An AI researcher under the same tests

The pitch behind AI trading tools is that a model can turn ideas into strategies in seconds. The problem is that a machine trying many ideas finds good-looking noise. So I gave an AI researcher the same referee: it writes each idea as code, a sandbox checks and runs it, a look-ahead test recomputes it on truncated data, a novelty test compares it with every published signal, and it only ever sees results for 1963–2004. The years 2005–2024 stay sealed until the end. Random search over the same data is the control.

Neither found anything. The AI's ideas were more original (84% new against 61%), but both show the same winner's curse: their best ideas averaged about 0.5 on the data they were chosen on and 0.10 and 0.14 on the sealed years.

11How it is built

The project is built to be checked by someone else: every result can be rebuilt from scripts in the repository, licensed data stays local, and the inputs are recorded with checksums.

How it is built
PartDetail
CodePython, about 5,000 lines; 30 unit tests on synthetic data, run by GitHub Actions on every push
