Macro Regime Predictor

A three-layer system that reads macro data and the words of central bankers, the press, companies and officials, labels the regime and trades government bonds on it.

  • 0.35Sharpe ratio of regime-timed duration, 1965–2025, against 0.17 for always long
  • +8%in 2022, when a long bond position lost 13%
  • 5regimes, each a rule on public data, labelled monthly from 1960
  • 4 × 7tiers of text and scores per document in the layer still to be tested

The Macro Regime Predictor is built in three layers. The first labels the macroeconomic regime from public data, using rules a person can read and only the figures that existed on the day. The second uses a language model to read central bank communication, news, interviews, bank research, earnings calls and policymakers' posts, and turns each document into seven dated scores. The third combines regime and text into positions in Treasury futures and curve trades, sized by regime. The first layer has been backtested on public data to the end of 2025; the text layer has not. This page gives the design, the test and what it showed.

01The system in one picture

The project began with a question from a conversation with a professor: could a language model read what moves rates, and could interpretable machine learning turn that into a bond strategy? The answer I built has three layers, and the order matters. The regime comes first, from data, so that the text is read in context: a hawkish speech means one thing late in a cycle and another in a recession.

The five regimes and the rule for each
RegimeRule, on real-time data
R1 Expansion, anchored inflationSahm indicator under 0.25; core inflation between 1.5% and 2.5%; manufacturing survey above 50
R2 Late cycle, inflation driftingSahm under 0.5; core inflation above 2.5% and rising; unemployment near its low
R3 Recession or risk-offSahm at or above 0.5, or a spike in jobless claims, or high-yield spreads above the past year's 95th percentile
R4 Recovery, disinflationSahm falling from its peak; core inflation falling; manufacturing survey crossing 50 from below
R5 StagflationCore inflation above 3.5%; long-run breakevens above 2.75%; unemployment rising

02Everything that is said

Policy is made in words before it shows up in numbers. The second layer reads those words wherever they appear: what central bankers say in statements, speeches and interviews; what the financial press and the wires report; what banks and economists write in research and newsletters; what company executives tell analysts; and what officials post in public.

Each document is scored on the same seven fields, so a Fed speech and an earnings call end up on one scale. The scores are then aggregated into daily series by tier, because a governor's speech and a commentator's column should not carry the same weight.

03Data that does not know the future

Macro data is revised. A label built on today's figures for 2008 contains information that was not available in 2008. So every observation is stored with two dates: the period it describes and the day it was published. A backtest sees only the version that existed on the day it trades. This layer was built first, before any regime label, because adding it later means redoing everything that rests on it.

The thresholds are not fitted. Each comes from published work or a policy target, such as 0.5 on the Sahm indicator and 2% on inflation, and each is then moved 20% either way to see whether the labels hold.

04From regime to trade, and how it is tested

The third layer puts macro and text features into a random forest and produces positions in government bonds. It trades duration and the shape of the curve, not single stocks: the brief was fixed income, where the analysis is harder and the edge is less crowded.

The labels cover 1960 to 2025. The trading test must contain three different breaks out of sample: the financial crisis, the pandemic and the inflation shock of 2022. A strategy that only works in one of them has learned that episode, not the cycle.

05A first test: layer one on its own

Layer one can be tested on its own, and I have. The classifier in the repository was run unchanged on public data from 1960 to the end of 2025: unemployment and payrolls from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, core PCE from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, industrial production and Treasury yields from the Federal Reserve. Each month it sees only data that had been published by then, and the label decides the position for the following month.

The positions were written down before any return was computed: long the ten-year bond in recession, half long in expansion and recovery, half short late in the cycle, short in stagflation. Size is scaled to a 10% volatility target and each trade costs 2 basis points.

The result is modest and real. Timing duration by regime earned a Sharpe ratio of 0.35, against 0.17 for holding the same bond at the same scaling throughout. With a curve trade added it is 0.47. That sits inside the 0.3 to 0.7 range I set in advance for a single market, which is some evidence it is not a leak. The clearest episode is 2022: the rules had the book short while inflation drifted up, and it made 8% in a year when a long position lost 13%.

06What the test told me to fix

The test also showed me what is wrong with version 0.1, and that is as useful as the Sharpe ratio.

  • The recession regime fires too often. It covers 33% of all months, when official recessions cover about 13%. The credit trigger is the cause: with monthly Baa spreads standing in for daily high-yield spreads, any new twelve-month high counts as stress.
  • High but falling inflation is called expansion. In mid-2022, with core inflation near 5%, a single month's dip moved the label from late cycle back to anchored expansion. The rule needs a level test as well as a direction test.
  • Labels flip too much. The average spell lasts 4.3 months. A regime should not change that often; the next version needs a confirmation rule.
  • Learning the positions does worse than stating them. Letting each regime's past returns set the position gives 0.18, below the stated priors. With so few regimes, economic reasoning beats estimation, which is what the design assumed.
  • The data is not yet true vintage. This run uses today's revised figures with conservative publication lags, not the archived first releases. Unemployment is barely revised; core inflation is, a little. The vintage run comes next.
  • No text. Layers two and three are not in this test. Whether the scores from speeches, news and calls add to this baseline is the question the project exists to answer.

07The problem with hidden regimes

Classic regime-switching models estimate a latent state and leave you to work out what it means. The result is the question every macro pitch book eventually meets: what, exactly, is regime two?

The framework this project builds on answers it by construction. A decision tree splits history on observable macro variables, choosing the split that best explains how the yield curve behaves on each side. The regimes that come out are rules: the policy rate is high, or it is low and inflation is high, or both are low.

08What switches: the shape of the curve

The model does not track ten yields separately. It tracks three factors that describe the whole curve: its level, its slope and its curvature. Regimes change how these three factors move and how they respond to capacity utilisation, the policy rate and inflation.

That choice keeps the model about something an economist can reason with, the shape of the curve, and not about ten loosely related numbers.

09The result that drives the strategy

The finding that matters for trading is about when macro information is useful at all. In the high-rate regime, macro variables forecast the curve beyond what the curve already contains. In the two low-rate regimes they add almost nothing: the curve has priced them in.

So the regime sets the size of the bet. Macro and text-derived signals are scaled up when rates are high and scaled down when they are not.

10Text as a macro variable

The second layer treats language as data. A language model reads Fed speeches, corporate filings and news, and scores each document for tone, novelty and policy conflict. The scores are aggregated into a panel by country and month, with every feature lagged to its real publication date.

A random forest then maps features to expected returns. Forests suit this job because there are many candidate features and weak theory about most of them; where theory is clear, a monotonic constraint holds the model to it.

11Testing without looking ahead

Every model is trained on the past and scored on the block of time that follows, then the window grows and the exercise repeats. Hyperparameters are chosen inside each training window. Nothing is shuffled, because shuffling time series lets the model see the future.

12The feature library

Before any model is fitted, the macro inputs are organised into nine families and each series is standardised in two ways: against the whole panel, so that markets can be compared, and against its own history, so that a series which is always high does not look permanently bullish.

13The bet, drawn

The text layer has no results yet, so this is its hypothesis as a picture, built from simulated data. A macro signal is assumed to have an edge only in the high-rate regime, which is what the reference paper finds for the yield curve. Trading it at one constant size wastes risk in the regimes where it knows nothing. Trading more in the first regime and less in the others, at the same total risk, should give the upper line.

Whether real signals behave this way is what the project is for.

14Setting expectations in advance

A project like this needs a yardstick before it has results, or any number will look good. The reference study behind the signal layer reports an average Sharpe ratio of 1.18 across eleven equity sectors, with single sectors between 0.12 and 0.73. That sets the range: around 1.0 to 1.5 for a diversified book of macro signals, 0.3 to 0.7 for one market alone.

Anything above 2 on a monthly macro signal is treated as an error to be found, not a result to be reported, until a permutation test says otherwise.

15What I designed around

  • Look-ahead in text. A document retrieved today for a date in 2015 may have been edited since. Each is timestamped to first publication.
  • A model that changes. Language models are updated. Versions are pinned, every extraction is cached, and a benchmark set is rescored to detect drift.
  • Few regime breaks. There are only four or five true breaks since 1990. Thresholds come from priors, and the claim made for any result is kept modest.
  • Uneven history. Call transcripts thin out before 2010 and social-media archives are costly, so central bank speeches carry the long history.
  • Too many states. Growth, inflation, credit and liquidity stacked together would make dozens of regimes that no sample could identify. One primary regime; the rest enter as features.

16What comes next

Three things, in order. Rerun the layer-one test on archived first-release data. Fix the three faults the test exposed: the credit trigger, the inflation rule and the flipping. Then build the text archive, score it, and measure whether it adds anything to the baseline above. That last comparison is the result the project is for.
