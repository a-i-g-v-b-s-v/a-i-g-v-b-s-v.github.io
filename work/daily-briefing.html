Daily FT and Economist briefing

A morning email that reads the Financial Times and The Economist, summarises every article and adds one macro view.

  • 18sections read every day: ten from The Economist, eight from the Financial Times
  • 07:05start of the run, on a schedule the laptop keeps by itself
  • 11readers, each sent their own message so one bad address cannot block the rest
  • 98.2%SIMof mornings delivered on time since the network fix

Each morning at 07:05 a scheduled job reads eighteen sections of the Financial Times and The Economist, keeps the newest five articles in each, writes a two-to-three-sentence summary of every one, adds a short macro analysis of the day and emails the result to a small list of family and colleagues. It is built to arrive, complete, before anyone is at a desk.

01The problem

Keeping up with macro and markets means reading two publications closely every day. Most of the value is in knowing what was published and which pieces deserve the full read. That triage is repetitive, so it is automated: every section, every morning, every article reduced to a few sentences in the style of a markets column.

02What is in the email

A table of contents grouped by publication, then each section with at most five articles. An article has a headline, a badge when the feed ranked it highly, and a summary of two or three sentences. The weekly publication gets a seven-day window so its pieces do not vanish after a day; the daily one gets twenty-four hours.

The summary is guaranteed. If the model fails on an article the feed's own description is used, and if that is empty the opening of the article is.

03Arriving on time

The hard part was never the summaries. It was the first thirty seconds. A laptop woken by a schedule at seven in the morning often has no network yet, so the first versions fetched nothing, or timed out halfway and sent a partial email.

The fix was plain engineering: check that the network is up before fetching, retry with exponential backoff, raise the time limit, and send to each reader separately.

04Under the hood

The script is 1,545 lines and most of them handle failures. It checks for a network connection before doing anything, signs in to each publication with credentials held in the system keychain, and gives every step a time limit so that one slow page cannot hold up the morning.

Summaries have three sources in order of preference, and sending has three attempts with a longer wait before each. Every reader gets a separate message, so one rejected address affects one person.

05A month of editions

The script logs to the console and keeps each day's output, but not a table of run statistics, so these charts are estimates of a typical month. An edition carries around seventy articles on a weekday and fewer at the weekend, when the daily paper publishes less. The run takes five to six minutes, a third of the fifteen it is allowed.

06What works today, and what does not

  • Works: the daily run, all eighteen sections, guaranteed summaries, the macro note, email that renders in Gmail and Outlook, and delivery to each reader independently.
  • Limits: it depends on one laptop being powered and online at seven, on the publications keeping their feeds, and on summaries of articles whose full text sits behind a paywall being written from the feed's description.
